Research-backed ways to settle stress. Try one that appeals, and skip anything that doesn’t feel comfortable.

Cyclic sighing1 to 5 minutes
  1. Breathe in through your nose, then add a second, smaller sip of air on top.
  2. Let a long, unhurried breath out through your mouth.
  3. Repeat for a few minutes, at whatever pace is easy.

In a 2023 Stanford trial, a few minutes of daily cyclic sighing improved mood more than mindfulness meditation. Sit or lie down, and stop if you feel lightheaded.

Progressive muscle relaxation5 to 15 minutes
  1. Start at your feet. Tense the muscles gently for about five seconds.
  2. Release fully and notice the difference for a few breaths.
  3. Work upward: calves, thighs, belly, hands, arms, shoulders, face.

Tense gently, and skip any area that is sore or injured. A long line of research links it to lower anxiety and better sleep.

5-4-3-2-1 grounding1 to 2 minutes
  1. Name 5 things you can see.
  2. 4 things you can feel, 3 you can hear.
  3. 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste.

It pulls attention out of racing thoughts and into the present moment, a common tool in anxiety care.

Body scan5 to 20 minutes

Lying or sitting, move your attention slowly from your head to your toes. Notice each area without trying to change it.

Mindfulness-based programs reliably reduce stress and rumination in controlled studies.

Name the feeling1 minute

Say or write what you feel in a few words, such as “I feel anxious about tomorrow’s meeting.”

Putting feelings into words (affect labeling) has been shown to calm the brain’s alarm response.

Three good things3 minutes, before bed

Write down three things that went well today and why each happened.

In a well-known trial, this simple habit raised happiness and lowered low mood for months.

Time in nature20 minutes

Spend time in a park, garden, or any green space. Leave your phone in your pocket and just look around.

One study found about 20 minutes outdoors was enough to lower the stress hormone cortisol.

General wellness ideas, not medical advice. If stress feels constant or overwhelming, talking with a doctor or therapist can help.

Important safety warnings

Never practice

  • While driving, riding, or running any vehicle or machinery.
  • In or near water: bath, shower, pool, lake or hot tub.
  • While standing or walking, or anywhere you could fall or be hurt if you faint.
  • Any time you need to stay alert.

Always

  • Sit or lie down in a safe, quiet place.
  • Breathe gently. Never force, strain, hold your breath, or breathe hard or fast.

Stop right away if you feel

  • Lightheaded, dizzy, tingling, numb, faint or panicky.
  • Chest pain or tightness, or shortness of breath.
  • Unwell in any way. Breathe normally and stay seated or lying down until it passes. For serious symptoms, get medical help (call 911 in the US, or your local emergency number).

Talk to a doctor first if you

  • Have heart or blood-pressure problems, asthma, COPD or other lung conditions.
  • Have epilepsy or seizures, or a history of fainting.
  • Are pregnant.
  • Have panic disorder or trauma that focusing on breath can trigger.
  • Take regular medication or have any medical concern.
  • Are under 18. Ask a parent or guardian and a doctor first.

Jolet is for general relaxation only. It is not medical or mental-health treatment and does not replace professional care. You use it at your own risk and are responsible for your own safety.
